AllPath — how it works

One lesson, no one left out

A walkthrough of what this project actually does, written to be read by someone who does not build software. Click the parts to open them. Nothing here needs the app to be running, and nothing here is a guess: where a claim comes from a file, the file is named.

What the app is for. A learner meets a wall that has nothing to do with their ability: a diagram they cannot see, a lesson they cannot hear, a passage written for a different reading level, an example from a place they have never been. AllPath takes a photo, a recording or a passage and produces a version adapted for one specific learner. A teacher checks everything before the learner sees it. That check is the whole point of this version.

One complete journey

A teacher photographs a diagram of the water cycle for a learner who is blind, checks what the AI produced, fixes a word, and releases it. This is the journey the version was built around.

  1. The teacher signs in. Teacher and learner share one tablet and have separate accounts. The password is turned into a hash on the device and the original is never stored or sent anywhere.
    components/LoginView.tsx · services/credentials.ts
  2. The learner's profile is read from the device. Language, grade, region, access need. It never left the tablet. In the previous version the profile was held in memory only and vanished on every reload.
    services/profileStore.ts
  3. They photograph the diagram. The photo and the profile go to this project's own server — without the learner's name, which is removed before the request is built.
    services/geminiService.ts
  4. The server asks the AI. AI step It builds the instructions — write in Kannada, Grade 6 reading level, this learner cannot see, so describe where things are rather than saying "look at this" — and sends them with the photo to Gemini. For the Easy Read mode it first asks a separate small service for a local analogy.
    server.ts · services/regionalContextMcpServer.ts
  5. The answer goes into a queue, not onto a screen. This single step is the version. Previously the answer went straight to the learner's screen the instant it arrived. Now it waits, marked "pending", and the learner's screen can only show items marked "released".
    services/reviewQueue.ts · services/generateForReview.ts
  6. The teacher reviews it. Photo on the left, the AI's text on the right and editable. They can release it, fix it and release it, or throw it away. A timer runs from opening the item to the decision.
    components/ReviewView.tsx
  7. They change "left" to "above" and press Release. Three things happen at once: the learner's version becomes the teacher's edited text, not the AI's original; the photo is deleted from the device; and one row is written to a log.
    services/reviewQueue.ts · services/reviewLog.ts
  8. The learner signs in and sees it. Only released items. Follow-up questions are switched off unless someone turns them on.
    App.tsx · components/ResultView.tsx

What happens when something goes wrong

The interesting part of any system. Four failures that matter here.

The AI call fails — and this one nearly bit us

If the model cannot be reached, or the key has run out of quota, the server replies with built-in sample text so the app still demonstrates. Until recently nothing said so: the sample text for Easy Read is the same rice-paddy analogy the lookup service hardcodes, so a failed call looked exactly like a successful showcase of the feature.

Why that was dangerous. A rate limit during a demonstration would have put invented content in front of a teacher, into the log, and into a research paper, labelled as something the AI produced.

Now every answer records where it came from. Sample text gets a warning banner on the review screen, is marked in the log, and is never stored in the cache — so a fabrication cannot outlive the failure that produced it. During measurement the server refuses to invent anything at all and simply fails.

server.ts · specs/review-spec.md section 10e
The same page is photographed twice

The second time, the stored answer is reused instead of paying for the model again. That saves time and money — but the reused answer still goes through review. The saving is the AI call, never the teacher's decision.

The stored answer is filed under a fingerprint of the photo, the mode, the learner's profile settings, and the AI model's version. Change any of those and it is a different question deserving a fresh answer. The model version is in there for a specific reason: without it, changing the model would silently serve old answers into the comparison a paper would rest on.

And the cache switches off during measurement. Each test case is meant to run three times, because the AI does not say the same thing twice and one run tells you about one run. A cache returns one answer three times — the variation you are trying to measure would vanish and the numbers would look beautifully consistent and mean nothing.

A teacher throws something away

The learner never sees it, the photo is deleted, and the log still records that it happened and that the decision was "discarded". A rejection is evidence too — if teachers discard a third of what the AI produces, that is the most important number in the study.

Someone tries to reach the review screen as a learner

They cannot. The control is not drawn for a learner account, and the screen is not reachable by routing either — two separate guards. This is checked by a test that renders the whole app as a learner and asserts the AI's text appears nowhere on the page.

Investigating a result that looks wrong

Suppose a teacher says a description was wrong about the diagram. What can you find out?

The log holds, for each decision: which mode, when it was generated, when it was decided, the decision, whether the teacher edited the text, how many seconds the review took, whether the answer came from the cache, and whether it was built-in sample text rather than AI output. You can export it as a spreadsheet.

What the log cannot tell you: it does not keep the text, the photo, or the prompt. The photo is deleted at the decision by design, and the log carries no learner's name. So the log can tell you that an item was edited and how long it took — it cannot show you what was wrong.

That is a deliberate trade of evidence for privacy, and it is worth stating out loud rather than discovering mid-study. If a study needs the text, that is a specification change with a consent question attached, not a small code edit.

How we know the checks are worth anything

A test suite that stays green when you break the thing it protects is worth nothing. So the protection was broken on purpose, three times, on a throwaway copy:

What was brokenWhat happened
The learner's screen stopped filtering for released items9 checks failed
A reused answer arrived already released2 checks failed
Sample text was allowed into the cache2 checks failed

Each break was undone afterwards. That is the evidence that the rules are enforced rather than merely described.

Check your understanding

A reused answer comes back from the cache. Does the learner see it straight away?

No. A cache hit saves the AI call, not the teacher's decision — it enters the queue as pending like anything else. Even if an identical item was released last week, this one is a new item and needs its own decision.

Why is the AI model's version part of the cache fingerprint?

So that changing the model cannot serve old answers as if they were new. The study measures one fixed system; a cache that ignored the version would quietly mix two.

A teacher reports a wrong description. Can you see the text they reviewed?

No, and that is by design. The log records that an item was edited, how long review took, and whether it was AI output — but not the text, and the photo was deleted at the decision. Privacy was chosen over evidence here, on purpose.

Words used here

Cache — a store of previous answers, so the same question does not have to be paid for twice.

Hash — a one-way scramble of a password. You can check a guess against it; you cannot recover the password from it.

Queue — a waiting line. Here, work waiting for a person to decide on it.

Model / the AI — Gemini, Google's system that reads the photo and writes the adapted text. It runs on Google's computers, not on the tablet.

Protocol — an agreed way for two programs to talk. The local-analogy service uses a standard one so other tools could call it.

Release — the teacher's act of letting a learner see something. The one door between the AI and a child.
